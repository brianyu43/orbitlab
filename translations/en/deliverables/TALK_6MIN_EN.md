# OrbitLab: six-minute presentation script

This draft gives roughly one minute to each slide. Adjust the timing to your speaking pace and the time you spend on the figures.

[0:00–1:00] OrbitLab asks whether a model built to handle rotations can reconstruct and generate images better with limited data. We used a small synthetic world of Ls, Ts, arrows, and zigzags. The two approaches had different strengths: B1, a conventional autoencoder trained with rotation augmentation, reconstructed images better at matched training time; B2, with an explicit C4 structure, followed generation conditions more often. Yet many B2 generations were still blurry or had the wrong shape. I will distinguish three questions: Is the rotation structure correct? Are pixels reconstructed well? Are new conditional samples good? We ran 29 AE comparison trainings, six additional AEs for generation, and eight flow trainings. The original “15 sessions” describes work items, not 15 days of training. [Pause at the figure, then advance.]

[1:00–2:00] There are four shapes and six colors. We left four shape–color combinations out of training as combination-OOD cases. Rotating the entire image by 90 degrees also rotates its position; each **column** in the figure shows four rotations of the same scene. We kept all rotations of a base scene in one split and removed duplicate rasters and rotation-orbit hashes. All 4,864 scenes have distinct orbits. The main training set has 1,024 scenes; validation, test, and OOD each have 256. We used one data seed and three initialization seeds, so these experiments do not measure uncertainty across possible data distributions. Standard deviation across initialization seeds and a scene-level bootstrap confidence interval describe different sources of variation. That distinction keeps the later numbers in perspective.

Sources: `data/manifest.json`, `tests/test_research.py`, `reports/ae_summary.json`.

[2:00–3:00] The vertical axis is foreground mean absolute error, so lower is better. In the main experiment, B1 and baseline B2 both trained for 3,000 steps, but they differ in parameter count and time: B1 has about 315,000 parameters and B2 about 118,000. Widening B2 to about 319,000 lowers its error, but training takes about 55 seconds. The two bars on the right show a separate matched-time comparison. In about 26.94 seconds, B1 completed roughly 5,667 steps and B2 about 2,765. Foreground MAE was 0.0446 versus 0.0727, favoring B1. B2 also did not consistently win with the small 256-scene training set. In this implementation, the hypothesis that structural equivariance necessarily gives a data-efficiency advantage is unsupported. The report’s figure shows the underlying seed values and standard deviations.

Sources: `reports/ae_results.csv`, `reports/ae_summary.json`, `reports/figures/fairness.png`.

[3:00–4:00] The C4 representation has four group slots, which we can decompose into Fourier components. The `m0` component is rotation-invariant. From top to bottom, this figure shows the input, reconstruction from the full latent, and reconstruction with **only `m0` retained**. The star-like or blob-like image in the bottom row illustrates lost direction and position. For seed 0, foreground MAE rose from 0.078 to 0.291. This does not prove that `m0` contains “meaning” and the other components contain only “pose”: deleting components can place the latent outside the decoder’s training distribution. The latent linear shape probe reached only about 42–45% accuracy, while color was easy to read. For the conventional AE, we evaluated rotation control with `A90` fitted on training data, rather than an arbitrary shift of slots. Rotation consistency and reconstruction quality have to be measured separately.

Sources: `reports/C4_MATH_KO.md`, `reports/representation_results.csv`, `runs/primary_equivariant_n1024_s0/analysis/band_interventions.json`. Mathematical background: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/cohenc16.html

[4:00–5:00] Before generation, we checked validation quality gates. One original B1 seed failed the color gate, so we trained both model families again for 6,000 steps without overwriting earlier results. We froze those AEs, trained the flows, and compared the **same 576 noise samples** at the Gaussian start and after 16, 32, and 64 ODE steps. The graph shows simultaneous shape-and-color accuracy for combinations seen in training. At 64 steps, B1 reached about 34% and B2 about 57%. The panel on the right shows the fixed first 48 samples, not hand-picked successes. Colors are often right, but some outlines and shapes are wrong. The evaluator was checked on clean synthetic shapes; blurry generations remain harder to judge. We therefore report a relative improvement without claiming that high-quality generation is solved. The report also includes OOD results, worst cases, and Gaussian-start images.

Sources: `reports/generation_summary.json`, `reports/quality_gate_repair.json`, `reports/state_evaluator_validation.json`. Flow Matching: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747

[5:00–6:00] Three conclusions. First, C4 symmetry held to numerical precision, but B1 reconstructed better at matched time. Second, the invariant component alone could not reconstruct images that need position and direction. Third, B2’s flow followed requested conditions more often, while generation quality remained incomplete. Pixel comparisons with nearest training images found no exact copies, but that cannot rule out memorization or missing parts of the distribution. Coverage here measures only a position grid pooled across conditions. We preserved the original materials, settings, data, code, checkpoints, CSVs, environment lock, and reproduction commands. The next priorities are a more robust image evaluator, repeated data seeds, and a comparison of total AE-plus-flow cost—not simply a larger model. We can pursue these questions within the limits this experiment establishes.
